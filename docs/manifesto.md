# The Machine Does Not Fear Your Anger. It Fears Your Coordination.

The machine is not technology. It is not one corporation, one government, one political party, or one rich asshole petting a hairless cat in a control room.

The machine is the system we created that turns human life into inputs and then pretends the result is inevitable.

It is the combination of ownership, bureaucracy, markets, law, media, debt, status, and habit. It decides who eats, who rests, who receives care, who gets heard, and who gets discarded. It can destroy a person without anyone involved ever deciding that person should be destroyed. Everyone can simply follow policy, meet their quota, protect their position, and go home believing they did nothing wrong.

That is what makes the machine so powerful. A tyrant can be removed. A machine has a thousand offices, a million passwords, and no single person responsible for what it does.

It does not need to hate you.

It only needs your suffering to be cheaper than changing what causes it.

I am not nostalgic for the past. The past was mostly disease, monarchy, racism, sexism, religious persecution, child labor, and dying because somebody sneezed near your drinking water. I am not trying to return to a cabin with no antibiotics and a king two valleys over. Modern society has produced extraordinary things. Medicine can restart a heart, replace an organ, and clean blood when the kidneys stop working. We can speak across the planet instantly. We can produce enough food, shelter, knowledge, and medicine to give nearly everyone a decent life.

The obscenity is that we have the capacity and still choose the suffering.

Or, more accurately, the machine chooses it through us, and we keep clocking in.

Capitalism is not the entire machine, but it is one of its largest engines. It takes whatever human beings need and asks how ownership can be inserted between the need and its fulfillment. Shelter becomes real estate. Health becomes insurance. Knowledge becomes intellectual property. Attention becomes advertising inventory. Friendship becomes engagement. Loneliness becomes a subscription opportunity.

Markets are useful tools. They are disastrous moral authorities.

The machine can sell you an anti-capitalist shirt, finance it in four payments, track you through the store's app, and ask whether you would recommend the experience to a friend.

It is incredibly adaptable.

Under this system, abundance does not necessarily eliminate scarcity. Sometimes abundance makes enforced scarcity more profitable. Homes can sit empty while people sleep outside. Food can be destroyed while people go hungry. Medicine can exist while patients die because the correct sequence of paperwork did not occur. People can work full time and still be unable to afford the place where they sleep between shifts.

Then the machine explains that these are personal failures.

You should have worked harder. Chosen a better career. Bought a house earlier. Avoided getting sick. Avoided being born disabled. Avoided being born in the wrong neighborhood. Avoided needing help at the exact moment help became necessary.

The machine takes a structural failure, delivers it to one person at a time, and calls it individual responsibility.

Personal responsibility does matter. I know that from recovery. Nobody else could make my choices for me. Nobody else could become honest for me, stay clean for me, or rebuild my life for me. But responsibility does not happen in a vacuum. It happens inside a body, a family, a neighborhood, an economy, and a set of available choices.

A person with food, housing, medical care, safety, and people who answer the phone has more freedom to act than a person who is starving, withdrawing, traumatized, sleeping outside, and being chased by police.

That is not an excuse. It is reality.

Pretending circumstances do not affect choices does not defend free will. It makes free will meaningless. Freedom requires more than being technically permitted to do something. Freedom requires having a real path to do it.

You are not meaningfully free to quit an abusive job when your medication, housing, and children's food depend on keeping it. You are not meaningfully free to leave an abusive partner when leaving means homelessness. You are not meaningfully free to reject surveillance when employment, banking, communication, and ordinary social existence require participation in systems that monitor you.

Consent requires a viable no. A checkbox saying "I agree" does not create consent when refusal means exile from modern life.

The machine also consumes time. Not just working hours, but the hours spent recovering from work, preparing for work, traveling to work, worrying about work, and proving that you deserve to keep working. It extracts the energy required to resist it and then sells exhausted people courses on time management.

It will schedule a mandatory webinar about burnout during lunch.

It will teach resilience instead of removing the thing people need to be resilient against.

This is part of what Marx meant by alienation. The worker becomes separated from the product of the work, the purpose of the work, the people around them, and eventually from themselves. Your time belongs to someone else. What you create belongs to someone else. The decisions belong to someone else. You are allowed to bring your "whole self" to work as long as that self remains productive, agreeable, and available on Slack.

Alienation does not only exist in factories. It exists whenever people lose meaningful control over what they do and why they do it.

Teachers are alienated when teaching becomes standardized testing and documentation. Medical workers are alienated when care becomes billing, throughput, and liability management. Artists are alienated when creativity becomes content production. Parents are alienated when they spend nearly every waking hour earning enough money to pay other people to care for their children.

The work may still matter. The people doing it may still care deeply. That is what makes the theft so complete. The machine does not merely take labor. It takes the human meaning inside the labor and turns that meaning into a performance metric.

In a famous English translation of Max Weber, another part of this becomes the “iron cage.” Modern institutions organize everything through rules, classifications, credentials, and procedures. Some of that protects us. Written rules can limit arbitrary power. Records can establish accountability. Standards can keep bridges standing and medicine sterile.

But bureaucracy becomes monstrous when following the procedure matters more than producing a humane result.

The person behind the counter may agree that the outcome is absurd. Their supervisor may agree. The policy writer may agree. Everyone agrees, and nothing changes, because agreement is not authority.

Nobody decided to deny the treatment. The treatment was simply not approved.

Nobody decided to make the family homeless. The payment was simply late.

Nobody decided that the disabled person should spend forty hours proving they are still disabled. The review was simply required.

The passive voice is the native language of the machine.

The machine does not require monsters. It requires ordinary people who stop asking whether an act is right and ask only whether it is authorized.

Foucault understood that power becomes even stronger when it no longer needs constant physical force. People learn to monitor themselves. We perform for employers, schools, governments, social groups, credit agencies, and invisible audiences. We track our productivity, attractiveness, employability, creditworthiness, popularity, and moral acceptability.

Eventually the guard does not need to watch us. We carry the guard around in our heads.

We ask whether rest has been earned. Whether joy is productive. Whether a hobby can be monetized. Whether a friendship is useful. Whether we are falling behind people we do not know, living lives we only see through selected photographs.

The machine colonizes the inner world by teaching us to measure ourselves using its categories.

This is where the digital machine becomes especially dangerous. The internet could have been a vast commons of human knowledge and connection. Parts of it still are. But much of modern digital life is controlled by companies whose product is behavior.

The system studies what keeps us staring, clicking, buying, fighting, and returning. Calm understanding is often less profitable than fear, humiliation, envy, tribal loyalty, and rage. So the machine gives each of us a personalized reality designed to keep the nervous system slightly activated.

It does not care whether we love what we see.

It cares whether we keep looking.

Even resistance becomes content. The machine can metabolize outrage into advertising revenue. It can turn every protest into a trend, every tragedy into a spectacle, and every moral conviction into a public performance. We feel as though we participated because we posted the right sentence under the right video.

Then nothing material changes.

The machine does not fear your opinion. It fears your coordination.

It does not care whether millions of isolated people are furious. Isolated fury is one of its most renewable resources. Furious people click more, buy impulsively, distrust one another, and mistake emotional exhaustion for political participation.

The machine fears people who compare notes, meet regularly, make specific demands, share resources, protect one another from retaliation, and refuse to resume normal operations until something changes.

That kind of rage requires more than emotion. It requires philosophy.

Stoicism matters, but not the cheap corporate version of Stoicism where workers are told to control their reactions while executives control their lives. Stoicism is not obedience. It is not emotional anesthesia. It does not mean accepting injustice as good or inevitable.

The useful Stoic question is: What is actually mine to govern?

My judgment is mine. My conduct is mine. My willingness to tell the truth is mine. My decision to cooperate, refuse, leave, help, or endure is mine. The outcome is not entirely mine. Other people are not mine. The past is not mine.

That distinction keeps me from wasting all my energy on what I cannot directly control. It does not tell me to do nothing. It tells me where action is still possible.

Courage and justice were Stoic virtues too. A philosophy that only teaches acceptance and forgets justice has been amputated for management purposes.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy offers another necessary distinction. Thoughts are real events, but they are not commands. Feelings contain information, but they do not automatically tell us what action will serve us. Rage can tell me that a boundary has been crossed. It cannot, by itself, tell me whether to organize, speak, leave, fight, rest, or burn down my own life.

The goal is not to eliminate rage. The goal is to carry it without letting it choose for us.

Acceptance does not mean approval. It means refusing to waste energy pretending the present moment is not happening. I can accept that I am afraid and still act. I can accept that the institution is powerful and still resist it. I can accept that change may be slow without accepting that change is impossible.

A wall does not disappear because I hate it.

But I cannot find the door by slamming my head into the wall.

Camus understood rebellion as the moment a person says, "No. This goes no further." That no also contains a yes. It says there is something in human beings that deserves protection. A rebel does not merely say, "Do not do this to me." The deeper claim is, "This should not be done to anyone."

That is where rage becomes solidarity.

Without solidarity, rage becomes narcissistic. It becomes my pain, my enemy, my revenge. It can reproduce the same hierarchy it claims to oppose. It can turn people into categories and justify cruelty because the correct people are now administering it.

I have no interest in replacing one machine with another machine painted the right color.

Our internal systems matter because dysregulated people are easier to divide and control. A terrified nervous system narrows its world. It looks for immediate threats, immediate relief, and simple enemies. That response can keep a person alive during acute danger. Under chronic pressure, it can also make long-term cooperation nearly impossible.

People who are permanently exhausted become easier to divide. People who are ashamed isolate themselves. People who are afraid cling to authority. People who are overwhelmed attack whoever is close enough to reach.

Self-regulation is not surrender. It is how we remain capable of choosing our actions.

Sleep matters. Food matters. Recovery matters. Friendship matters. Quiet matters. Humor matters. A body cannot remain in a permanent state of emergency without eventually paying for it.

This is not the wellness industry's version of self-care, where we buy scented candles so we can tolerate another quarter of exploitation. It is maintenance for resistance. We protect the nervous system because we need it. We protect attention because attention determines what kind of life we are able to build.

The machine wants us to believe that rest is laziness and exhaustion is commitment.

Fuck that.

Rest is where judgment returns. It is where imagination returns. It is where we remember that survival is supposed to serve life, not replace it.

The machine also divides us by turning the people beside us into competitors. Worker against worker. Citizen against immigrant. Employed against unemployed. Renter against homeless person. Sober person against addict. College graduate against the person who learned outside school. One generation against another. One race against another. Men against women. Rural people against urban people.

Some of these groups experience different kinds and degrees of harm. Unity cannot mean pretending power is distributed equally. The cogs are not identical. Some are being ground apart. Some are kept comfortable. Some own part of the machine.

Solidarity does not require denying those differences.

It requires refusing to let the differences become excuses for abandonment.

We can recognize that racism targets people differently without believing poor people of other races have no common interests. We can recognize sexism without treating every man as the author of patriarchy. We can protect LGBTQ people without requiring every potential ally to arrive with perfect language and a completed degree in social theory. We can hold people accountable without treating every mistake as proof that they are permanently contaminated.

A movement that burns every imperfect participant is just another sorting machine wearing a radical badge.

Unity does not mean total agreement. It means deciding that certain human needs and freedoms are more important than the disagreements the machine uses to isolate us.

Everyone should have food.

Everyone should have shelter.

Everyone should have healthcare.

Everyone should have meaningful control over their own body and life.

Everyone should be able to leave an abusive employer, landlord, partner, platform, or institution without losing the ability to survive.

Everyone should have a voice in the systems that govern them.

That is enough common ground to organize a civilization around.

Productive rage begins with concrete relationships. Not followers. Not audiences. People who know one another well enough to share risk.

A coworker shares salary information instead of letting management negotiate with everyone in secret. Workers document abuse together. Tenants compare rent increases. Neighbors watch each other's children. Someone accompanies a scared person to court. Someone teaches another person how to appeal a denial. People build strike funds before a strike. Communities feed families before asking them to take heroic risks.

This is mutual aid. It is not charity delivered from the good people to the unfortunate people. It is recognition that vulnerability moves between us. Today I have what you need. Tomorrow you may have what I need. We survive by refusing the fiction that independence means never depending on anyone.

Recovery taught me that no one escapes alone.

The individual still has to make the choice. But other people can keep the door visible long enough for the choice to become possible.

Collective action works the same way. Anger without a target is heat. Anger with a target, a demand, leverage, and people willing to remain together becomes pressure.

A protest should know what it wants. A boycott should know what would end it. A strike needs support for the people who cannot afford to miss a paycheck. Civil disobedience needs people handling food, transportation, legal defense, communication, and care. Not everyone has to stand in the same place or take the same risk.

Movements fail when they confuse visibility with power. Ten thousand people yelling for one afternoon may matter, but a hundred organized workers who can stop a critical operation may have more leverage. A viral post can expose a problem. It cannot negotiate a contract, stop an eviction, run a food pantry, or sit with someone through withdrawal.

The work is usually boring before it becomes historic.

Meetings. Phone calls. Food. Childcare. Records. Arguments about wording. Learning who actually has authority. Finding the rule. Showing up again after the news cycle moves on.

The machine loves a rebellion that ends at brunch.

It struggles more with people who keep returning.

We also have to admit that we are inside the machine. Most of us enforce some part of it. We follow policies we know are harmful. We buy things produced by exploitation. We remain silent when speaking would cost us. We pass pressure downward because we cannot safely push it upward.

That does not make everyone equally responsible. Power matters. The employee denying a claim under threat of losing their job does not have the same responsibility as the executive who designed the denial system and receives a bonus when fewer claims are paid.

But being a cog does not eliminate agency.

The clerk can explain the appeal.

The manager can refuse an impossible quota.

The coworker can share what management said privately.

The neighbor can refuse to call the police when someone needs help rather than punishment.

The person with more safety can absorb more risk.

The person with knowledge can make it available.

The person with money can fund the people doing the work.

The person with a platform can point attention toward an organization instead of making themselves the center of the story.

We do not need everyone to become a hero. We need millions of people to become slightly less obedient to cruelty and much more loyal to one another.

The machine depends on us believing that everyone else will comply. It depends on each worker assuming the others will remain silent, each tenant assuming the others will pay, each citizen assuming the others will stay home, and each suffering person assuming their suffering proves a private defect.

Once people compare notes, the illusion starts to fail.

"Oh. It is not just me."

That sentence has started more rebellions than any slogan.

The final danger is becoming what we hate. Rage can make domination feel righteous. It can make humiliation feel like justice. It can make cruelty feel acceptable when directed at people who represent the system.

But a person is not a symbol. A person may carry power, enforce harm, and deserve resistance. They are still a person.

The point is not to reverse the direction of dehumanization. The point is to stop organizing society around it.

I do not want a kinder machine that still owns us. I do not want oppression with inclusive branding. I do not want exploitation administered by people who use the correct vocabulary. I want systems that remain accountable to human life.

That means democratic workplaces, strong unions, cooperative ownership, public goods, accessible healthcare, housing that is treated as shelter before it is treated as an investment, and institutions where ordinary people can understand, challenge, and change the decisions governing them.

It means building alternatives while resisting what exists. A food cooperative will not abolish hunger by itself. A union will not end exploitation everywhere. A community clinic will not repair the entire healthcare system. But each creates a place where another way of living becomes materially real.

That matters.

People need proof that life can be organized differently. Not only arguments. Not only theories. Places where cooperation works, where dignity is normal, and where survival does not require submission.

The machine teaches us that nothing else is possible because everything around us has been arranged to confirm it.

Our job is to create counterexamples.

I rage against the machine because I have seen what happens to people underneath it. I have been homeless, addicted, jailed, traumatized, and sick enough to need an actual machine to keep me alive. The actual machine was not my enemy. It cleaned my blood.

The enemy was every human system that could classify suffering more easily than it could respond to it.

I also know that rage is not enough. Rage can wake us up, but it cannot decide where to go. We need courage without recklessness, acceptance without surrender, discipline without obedience, accountability without disposability, and unity without pretending we are all affected in the same way.

We need to stop asking whether one person can defeat the machine.

One person cannot.

One person can tell the truth. Another can confirm it. Ten can refuse. A hundred can protect the ten. A thousand can make the refusal expensive. Millions can make the old arrangement impossible.

The machine is enormous, but it is not a god. It is made of laws, contracts, budgets, habits, fears, incentives, and human participation.

It was built.

It can be changed.

Not by rage as performance. Not by purity. Not by waiting for a flawless leader. Not by sacrificing ourselves individually and hoping the spectacle inspires someone.

By coordination.

By refusing to let people disappear alone.

By building the systems we wish had existed when we needed them.

By protecting one another long enough to become dangerous together.

That is how we rage against the machine.

Not as isolated cogs screaming into the noise.

As human beings who finally recognize one another.
